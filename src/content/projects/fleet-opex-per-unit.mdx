## The problem

The fleet was growing, and total operating cost grew with it. That made total cost a poor signal: it rises whether operations are getting better or worse, so it cannot answer the question leadership actually asked, which was whether each additional machine was getting cheaper or more expensive to run.

## Why it mattered

Every placement decision, hiring decision, and routing decision has a service-cost consequence, and none of them were being compared on one scale. Without a shared denominator, an improvement in one place could not be told apart from growth somewhere else.

## What I built

A single metric, **Fleet OpEx per Unit (FOU)**: the all-in cost of servicing the fleet in a month, divided by the number of active machines. I defined it and I track it, and it is the number I use to judge whether an operational change was worth keeping.

FOU fell by 33%. The large movers were operational rather than financial: dispatch optimization and geographic clustering of stops, described in [Autonomous technician dispatch](/projects/dispatch-optimization). Because the metric is per unit, the result holds while the fleet nearly doubled from 15 to 28 machines, which total cost could never have shown.
